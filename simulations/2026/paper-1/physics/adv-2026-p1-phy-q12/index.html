03Detailed solution

Answer: n = —

  1. Slice the cone into rings. At a fraction u of the way along the slant (0 at the tip, 1 at the rim), the ring has radius r = uR. The curved area up to u grows as u², so a ring of width du holds charge dq = Q · 2u du.
  2. Each ring is a current loop. Spinning at ω, the charge passes any point ω/2π times a second, so dI = dq ω/2π. Its magnetic moment is
    dm = dI · πr² = (1/2) dq ω r² = QωR² u³ du.
  3. Add them up. m = QωR² ∫₀¹ u³ du = QR²ω/4. The height h drops out.
  4. Far along the axis, the cone looks like a dipole. For z ≫ R, h, the axial field of a dipole is
    B = (μ₀/4π) · 2m/z³ = (μ₀/4π) · QR²ω/(2z³)  ⇒  n = 0.5.
  5. What the experiment checks. The probe adds up the exact on-axis field of every ring, μ₀ dI r²/2(r² + (z − zring)²)3/2, without assuming a dipole. As z grows, Bz³ settles at the value that gives n = 0.5, for any height of cone.

Understand the concept

A rotating charged body is a stack of current loops. Far away, all that survives of their fields is the total magnetic moment. For a loop of radius r carrying charge dq at angular speed ω, the moment is dqωr²/2. The moment is not equal to the charge times the average r²: the charge sits mostly near the rim (dq ∝ u), where r² is largest, so the moment is weighted by ∫2u·u² = 1/2. On the axis, the far field of a dipole is (μ₀/4π)(2m/z³), twice its broadside value.

Key takeaways

  • A spinning charge dq on a ring of radius r has magnetic moment dq ω r²/2.
  • Uniform charge on a cone's curved surface: dq = 2Qu du with r = uR, which gives m = QR²ω/4.
  • On-axis dipole field: B = μ₀ 2m/(4πz³), so n = 0.5. The cone's height does not matter far away.
