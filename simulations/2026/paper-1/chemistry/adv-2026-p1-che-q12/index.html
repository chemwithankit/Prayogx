    Understand the concept

    Terminal alkynes are acids

    An sp C–H (50 % s-character) has pKa ≈ 25; NH₃ has pKa ≈ 38, so NH₂⁻ removes it completely.

    RC≡C–H + NH₂⁻ → RC≡C⁻ + NH₃

    Acetylides are nucleophiles

    The carbanion attacks a primary alkyl halide by SN2 — backside attack, one step, inversion at the CH₂.

    RC≡C⁻ + R'CH₂Br → RC≡C–CH₂R' + Br⁻

    sp carbons are linear

    An sp carbon holds its two σ-bonded neighbours at 180°. A chain of sp carbons is straight — and so are the two atoms at its ends.

    X–C≡C–C≡C–Y : all 6 on one axis

    The line stops at sp³

    The CH₂ is sp³ (109.5°): its next bond leaves the axis. The alkene beyond is sp² (120°).

    answer = 4 sp C + 2 CH₂ = 6

    1. Step 1 — Double deprotonation (2 equivalents of NaNH₂)

      Each NH₂⁻ lone pair (gold dots) takes a terminal proton; the C–H bond pair stays on carbon. Both ends react because 2 equivalents are used.
      The acetylide dianion ⁻C≡C–C≡C⁻ (2 Na⁺ counter-ions) and two molecules of NH₃ (pKa 38 ≫ 25, so the equilibrium lies fully to the right).
      On the bench: the grey NaNH₂ was used up as the diyne was bubbled in; the proton went to NH₃ (the solvent).

    2. Step 2 — SN2 alkylation at both ends (excess crotyl bromide)

      SN2: the acetylide carbon attacks the CH₂ from the side opposite to Br (backside attack); the C–Br bond pair leaves with Br⁻. Transition state [C···CH₂···Br]⁻, trigonal-bipyramidal carbon; the CH₂ group inverts like an umbrella.

      The same happens at the other end. Crotyl bromide is primary and allylic — ideal for SN2; with excess bromide both acetylide ends are alkylated. The C=C keeps its trans geometry because no bond to it is touched.

      On the bench: bromide dripped in and white NaBr settled as Br⁻ met Na⁺.

    3. Step 3 — The product X

      X = (2E,10E)-dodeca-2,10-diene-5,7-diyne, CH₃CH=CHCH₂–C≡C–C≡C–CH₂CH=CHCH₃ (C₁₂H₁₄). Highlighted: the six carbons on one straight line.
      ⁻C≡C–C≡C⁻ + 2 CH₃CH=CHCH₂Br → CH₃CH=CHCH₂–C≡C–C≡C–CH₂CH=CHCH₃ + 2 Br⁻

    4. Step 4 — Hybridisation of every chain carbon

      Carbon (from one end)GroupHybridisationAngle at this carbonOn the line?
      C1CH₃sp³—no
      C2, C3CH=CHsp²120°no
      C4CH₂sp³109.5°yes — bonded to an sp carbon along its axis
      C5–C8–C≡C–C≡C–sp180°yes
      C9CH₂sp³109.5°yes
      C10, C11CH=CHsp²120°no — the chain turns by 70.5° at C9
      C12CH₃sp³—no

    5. Step 5 — Count

      C4 – C5 ≡ C6 – C7 ≡ C8 – C9 : angles at C5, C6, C7, C8 = 180° ⇒ all six on one axis
      at C4 and C9 (sp³) the next bond turns off the axis by 180° − 109.5° = 70.5°
      maximum collinear carbons = 4 (sp) + 2 (the CH₂ on each end) = —

      Traps: counting only the sp carbons gives 4; letting the line run on past the CH₂ gives 8 or more; the trans C=C is not linear (120°).

    Independent verification

    • RDKit templates. Deprotonation and SN2 templates applied to buta-1,3-diyne and trans-crotyl bromide give X = C₁₂H₁₄ with the E,E geometry kept.
    • 3-D geometry. X embedded and optimised with MMFF: the largest set of carbons within 0.1 Å of one straight line is 6.
    • Controls. Hexatriyne gives 8, ethyne 4, one equivalent of base 5, allyl or propargyl bromide 6 — the same rule every time.
    • Solution websites. MathonGo's published solution (their Q44) gives 6.
    • This page. The arrows are pushed on the molecule graph; hybridisation is read from bond orders; the count is made twice (by rule and on the geometric model) at run time.
    • Answer key. Checked afterwards against the official final key printed with the paper: Q12 → 6.
