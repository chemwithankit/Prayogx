Question stem for question nos. 15 and 16

Two volatile liquids A and B form an ideal solution. Consider a 5 molal solution of B in A inside a closed container having a total vapour pressure of 100 mm Hg at 300 K. The vapour pressure of pure A at 300 K is 105 mm Hg. Assume that A and B behave as ideal gases in the vapour phase.

GivenValue
The gas constant R0.08 L atm K−1 mol−1
Molar mass of A50 g mol−1
Molar mass of B57 g mol−1
Density of liquid B at 300 K0.5 g/mL
1 atm760 mm Hg

Q.15  At 300 K, the ratio of the molar volume of pure B in vapour phase to its molar volume in liquid phase is ____ .

Read the phrase carefully. Molar volume of pure B in the vapour phase means one mole of B on its own, as a gas, at 300 K and at its own vapour pressure pB° — not at the total pressure of the mixture and not at 1 atm. Finding pB° is what the first half of the question is for.
The stem's other question, Q.16, asks for the mole fraction of B in the vapour phase. This page is Q.15 only; the vapour composition is left alone so it stays a fresh question.

06Step-by-step solution

Run the experiment and reveal the answer — the derivation opens with it.

The solution is still locked

The bench has not produced an answer yet. Enter the data, run the experiment through to the end and press REVEAL ANSWER — this opens by itself at the same moment.

  1. Molality is per kilogram of solvent, so take 1 kg of A as the basis

    A 5 molal solution has 5 mol of B for every 1000 g of A. Nothing else about the amount matters — the ratio of the two components is what the question fixes.

    n(A) = 1000 g ÷ 50 g mol−1 = 20 mol  ·  n(B) = 5 mol

  2. Convert to mole fraction

    Raoult's law is written in mole fractions, never in molality, so this conversion is the first real step of the problem.

    xB = 5 / (20 + 5) = 0.2  ·  xA = 1 − 0.2 = 0.8

    In closed form, xB = m / (m + 1000/MA), which shows that the mass of A you start from cancels out entirely.

  3. Raoult's law gives the vapour pressure of pure B

    For an ideal solution the total pressure is the sum of the two partial pressures, each of them the pure-component vapour pressure scaled by that component's mole fraction. Only pB° is unknown, so the equation solves for it.

    ptotal = pA° xA + pB° xB
    100 = (105)(0.8) + pB°(0.2) = 84 + 0.2 pB°
    0.2 pB° = 16  ⟹  pB° = 80 mm Hg

    A check that costs nothing: 105 × 0.8 = 84 and 80 × 0.2 = 16, and 84 + 16 = 100, the pressure that was measured.

  4. Molar volume of B as a liquid — from its density

    One mole of liquid B weighs 57 g, and 57 g of a liquid of density 0.5 g/mL occupies 57/0.5 mL. Density is the only property of the liquid that enters.

    Vm(liquid) = MB / ρB = 57 / 0.5 = 114 mL mol−1 = 0.114 L mol−1

  5. Molar volume of B as a vapour — from the ideal gas law at its own pressure

    Pure B as a vapour at 300 K sits at pB° = 80 mm Hg. Convert to atmospheres, because R is quoted in L atm K−1 mol−1.

    p = 80/760 atm  ·  Vm(vapour) = RT/p = (0.08 × 300) × (760/80) = 24 × 9.5 = 228 L mol−1

  6. The ratio

    Both volumes are per mole, so the units cancel and the answer is a pure number.

    Vm(vapour) / Vm(liquid) = 228 / 0.114 = 2000

    It is worth seeing why the number is so large. A mole of gas at this pressure is spread over roughly two hundred litres while the same mole of liquid is about a tenth of a litre: the molecules are the same, the spacing is not. In each direction the expansion is only ∛2000 ≈ 12.6, which is why the two cubes on the bench are drawn at that edge ratio rather than at 2000:1.

Independent verification

  • Derived first, checked afterwards. The mole fraction, Raoult step, both molar volumes and the ratio were worked out from the data alone before any published solution was consulted.
  • The basis mass is irrelevant, proved rather than assumed. Repeating the calculation on 500 g, 1000 g, 2500 g and 137 g of A gives xB = 0.2 every time, matching the closed form m/(m + 1000/MA). The simulation reproduces this live — sweeping the mass of A changes n(A) and n(B) but never moves the answer.
  • Raoult back-substitution. Putting pB° = 80 back in returns 105(0.8) + 80(0.2) = 100 mm Hg exactly, the pressure the question states.
  • The ratio computed by two independent routes. RT/p ÷ (MB/ρB) gives 2000, and the closed form R·T·(760/pB°)·ρB·1000 ÷ MB gives 2000 to machine precision.
  • Scaling laws checked away from the paper's numbers. Doubling T doubles the ratio, doubling pB° halves it, doubling the density of the liquid doubles it — exactly as RT ρ/(M p) requires. The model therefore behaves correctly over its whole range, not only at the question's values.
  • Dimensions and magnitude. L mol−1 over L mol−1 leaves a pure number. At 1 atm and 300 K the molar volume would be 24 L mol−1; at 80/760 atm it is 9.5 times larger, giving 228 — and a vapour near a tenth of an atmosphere sitting about 103 times a liquid's volume is the expected order of magnitude.
  • Distractor audit. Using ptotal instead of pB° gives 1600; using pA° gives 1523.81; forgetting the mm Hg → atm conversion gives 2.63; leaving the liquid volume in mL gives 2; reading 5 molal as xB = 5/20 gives 1882.35; inverting the ratio gives 5 × 10−4. None of them is 2000, and every one is reproduced on the bench as a run.
  • Headless-browser run of this page. The finished simulation was driven end to end in Chromium and asserted to reproduce 2000 from the live model, to produce different answers on different data, to refuse data that makes pB° negative, to keep the answer off the page until it is revealed, and to render without console errors or horizontal overflow at 390 px and 360 px.
  • Cross-check. Afterwards the answer was compared against a published worked solution for this paper, which follows the same route and reports the same 2000 with the same intermediate values (xB = 0.2, pB° = 80 mm Hg, 228 and 0.114 L mol−1). 07Concepts & classification

Five ideas carry this question, and the experiment used them in this order. The cards animate in when the answer is revealed — or right now, if you would rather read first.

The five concepts, in the order the bench used them

CONCEPT 01

Molality is not mole fraction

Molality counts moles of solute per kilogram of solvent; mole fraction counts them against all the moles present. Raoult's law is written in mole fractions, so a molality always has to be converted first — and the conversion needs the molar mass of the solvent, which is why it is given.

xB = m / (m + 1000/MA)  ⟹  …
CONCEPT 02

Raoult's law for an ideal solution

Each component escapes into the vapour in proportion to how much of the surface it owns. The partial pressure is the pure-component vapour pressure scaled by that component's mole fraction, and the total is their sum — a straight line between pA° and pB°.

ptotal = pA°xA + pB°xB  ⟹  …
CONCEPT 03

Molar volume of a liquid comes from density

A liquid's molecules are already touching, so its volume is set by how much mass you have and how tightly it packs. One mole is M grams, and M grams of a liquid of density ρ occupies M/ρ — no temperature, no pressure, no gas law.

Vm(liq) = MB/ρB  ⟹  …
CONCEPT 04

Molar volume of a vapour comes from the gas law — at its own pressure

"Pure B in the vapour phase" is one mole of B alone, at 300 K, at the pressure pure B actually exerts: the pB° the bench measured — not the mixture's total and not 1 atm. Convert to atmospheres, because R is quoted in L atm K−1 mol−1.

Vm(vap) = RT/pB°  ⟹  …
CONCEPT 05

Why the ratio is so large, and what it depends on

Both volumes are per mole, so the units cancel and the answer is a pure number. It rises with temperature, falls as the vapour pressure rises, and rises with the density of the liquid. In each direction the expansion is only the cube root of it.

Vvap/Vliq = RTρB·1000 / (MB·pB°)  ⟹  …

Formulas used

xB = nB / (nA + nB),   nA = wA/MA,   nB = m · wA/1000
ptotal = pA° xA + pB° xB  (Raoult, ideal solution)
Vm(liquid) = M / ρ
Vm(vapour) = RT / p,   p in atm = p(mm Hg)/760
ratio = RTρ·1000 / (M · pB°) — dimensionless

Notice what is not in the last expression: the mass of A, the molar mass of A and the molality appear only through pB°, and pA° and ptotal only through the same quantity. Sweep the mass of A on the bench and the answer does not move.
