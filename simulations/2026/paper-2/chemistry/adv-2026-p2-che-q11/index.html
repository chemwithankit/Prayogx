01The question — verbatim

At a given temperature, 0.45 g of acetic acid in 50 mL of water is shaken with 1.0 g of charcoal and the pH of the resulting solution is 3.0. Assume, the adsorption of acetic acid from the aqueous solution by charcoal follows Freundlich isotherm,
x/m = k C1/n
If the plot of log10(x/m) against log10C gives a straight line with slope 1, the value of k in L mol−1 is ______.
Given: molar mass of acetic acid = 60 g mol−1 · Ka = 1.0 × 10−5 · x = mass (g) of acetic acid adsorbed · m = mass (g) of charcoal · C = equilibrium concentration of acetic acid in solution after adsorption · k and n are constants for the acetic acid–charcoal system.

What this experiment is doing. Charcoal is riddled with pores, so one gram carries an enormous surface. Acid molecules that bump into it stick to that surface — they are adsorbed. Nothing is destroyed: the acid simply leaves the water and sits on the solid, so the solution ends up weaker than it started.

Why x/m and not just x. Two grams of charcoal would hold twice as much acid, so the raw mass adsorbed describes the amount of charcoal, not the material. Dividing by the mass of charcoal gives a number that belongs to the charcoal itself.

04The Freundlich plot

Take logarithms of the isotherm and the power law straightens into a line. Its gradient is the only thing left between the measurement and k.

Straightening the isotherm

x/m = k C1/n
log10(x/m) = log10k + (1/n) log10C

That is y = c + sx: the gradient s is 1/n and the intercept is log10k.

Why logarithms help. A power law is a curve, and eyeballing a curve tells you very little. Taking logs of both sides turns it into a straight line, and a straight line hands you two numbers at once: its gradient and where it crosses the axis.

06Step-by-step solution

The solution is still locked

Run the experiment above and the derivation opens by itself.
Steps completed: 0 of 7

  1. What went into the flask

    moles = 0.45 g ÷ 60 g mol−1 = 0.0075 mol
    Cinitial = 0.0075 mol ÷ 0.050 L = 0.15 mol L−1

    The volume has to be in litres. Dividing by 50 instead of 0.050 gives 0.00015, which is moles per millilitre.

  2. The pH reading is about H+, not about the acid

    [H+] = 10−pH = 10−3.0 = 1.0 × 10−3 mol L−1

    Acetic acid is weak: only a small fraction of it has given up a proton, so the hydrogen ions the meter sees are nowhere near the amount of acid present. That conversion is the whole point of being handed Ka.

  3. Ka turns [H+] into the acid concentration

    CH3COOH ⇌ CH3COO− + H+     Ka = [CH3COO−][H+] / [CH3COOH]

    Both ions come only from the acid, so [CH3COO−] ≈ [H+], and the expression collapses:

    Ka = [H+]² / C  ⟹  C = [H+]² / Ka
    C = (1.0 × 10−3)² ÷ (1.0 × 10−5) = 0.10 mol L−1

    Sense check: 0.10 M is below the 0.15 M you started with, which is exactly what adsorption should do.

  4. Mass balance — the acid moved, it did not vanish

    nleft = C × V = 0.10 × 0.050 = 0.005 mol
    mass left = 0.005 × 60 = 0.30 g
    x = 0.45 − 0.30 = 0.15 g adsorbed

    0.30 g in the solution plus 0.15 g on the charcoal is the 0.45 g weighed out. A third of the acid ended up on the surface.

  5. x/m

    x/m = 0.15 g ÷ 1.0 g = 0.15

    x is a mass, as the question states, so x/m is grams per gram — a pure number.

  6. The gradient gives n

    log10(x/m) = log10k + (1/n) log10C

    A straight line whose gradient is 1/n. The question hands you the gradient:

    1/n = 1  ⟹  n = 1  ⟹  x/m = kC

  7. k

    0.15 = k × (0.10)1  ⟹  k = 0.15 ÷ 0.10 = 1.5
    units: (g/g) ÷ (mol L−1) = L mol−1

    The C in the isotherm is the concentration left at equilibrium, 0.10 M — not the 0.15 M you started with. Using 0.15 gives 1.0 and is the commonest wrong answer here.

  8. Answer

    k = 1.5 L mol−1

    Marking: +4 for 1.5, 0 otherwise. No negative marking, so there is never a reason to leave this blank.

✓ Independent verification

  • Derived before anything was looked up. Every step — the mole calculation, the Ka inversion, the mass balance, the gradient and the final division — was fixed from first principles, and only then compared with a published solution.
  • The Ka route checked in both directions: forwards it gives C = 0.10 M from pH 3.00, and substituting back, [H+] = √(KaC) = 1.000 × 10−3, reproducing the meter reading exactly.
  • Conservation checked explicitly: 0.30 g in solution + 0.15 g adsorbed = 0.45 g weighed out, to machine precision. The simulation enforces the same identity frame by frame while the flask is shaking.
  • Two independent routes to k: dividing (x/m) by C gives 1.5, and reading the intercept of the log–log line gives log10k = 0.176091, i.e. k = 1.500000. Both are shown on the plot.
  • Units confirmed by dimensional analysis: (g g−1) ÷ (mol L−1) = L mol−1, which is the unit the question asks for — a check that x was correctly taken as a mass rather than a number of moles.
  • The traps reproduced deliberately: using the initial 0.15 M gives exactly 1.0; treating pH 3 as the acid concentration gives an absurd 150; dividing by 50 instead of 0.050 L gives 0.00015; putting x in moles gives 0.025 L g−1. None is 1.5.
  • The approximation quantified rather than assumed: solving the weak-acid equilibrium exactly, without treating the remaining acid as wholly undissociated, gives C = 0.1010 M and k = 1.455 — a 3.0% shift. The question's round numbers are chosen so the standard approximation lands on a clean 1.5, which is the intended answer.
  • Answer key cross-check: a published worked solution for this paper follows the same steps and reports the same intermediate values (10−3 M, 0.10 M, 0.005 mol, 0.30 g, 0.15 g, n = 1) and the same answer, 1.5 L mol−1. IIT Roorkee's official key was not retrievable at the time of writing.

07Concepts and classification

Relations used

moles = mass ÷ molar mass
C = moles ÷ volume in litres
[H+] = 10−pH
weak acid: Ka = [H+]² / C ⟹ C = [H+]²/Ka
mass balance: x = mass taken − mass left in solution
Freundlich: x/m = k C1/n
log form: log(x/m) = log k + (1/n) log C
gradient = 1/n  ·  intercept = log k
adsorption is a surface process — nothing leaves the flask

Teacher notes

  • Objective. Two chapters in one question: the ionic-equilibrium step that turns a pH into a concentration, and the surface-chemistry step that turns a concentration into k. Students who know both separately still lose this one at the join.
  • Runs as a demo. Press Auto run and the whole experiment plays through in about half a minute — long enough to narrate, short enough to repeat.
  • Stop the class at the pH step. Ask for the acid concentration at pH 3 before revealing Ka. Most will say 10−3 M. Then let the next step find 0.10 M — a hundred times bigger.
  • Expected wrong answers. 1.0 by far (using the initial 0.15 M in the isotherm); then 150 (treating pH as the acid concentration); occasionally 0.025 (putting x in moles).
  • Discussion question. "If we doubled the charcoal to 2.0 g, what would happen to x, to x/m, and to k?" (More acid adsorbed, a lower equilibrium C, but k unchanged — it is a constant of the system.)
  • Board demonstration. Shaking tea with a spoonful of activated charcoal decolourises it in under a minute. Same physics, and it lands.
