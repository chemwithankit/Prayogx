01Original question — verbatim

At a given temperature, 0.45 g of acetic acid in 50 mL of water is shaken with 1.0 g of charcoal and the pH of the resulting solution is 3.0. Assume, the adsorption of acetic acid from the aqueous solution by charcoal follows Freundlich isotherm,
x/m = k C1/n
If the plot of log10(x/m) against log10C gives a straight line with slope 1, the value of k in L mol−1 is ______.
Given: molar mass of acetic acid = 60 g mol−1 · Ka = 1.0 × 10−5 · x = mass (g) of acetic acid adsorbed · m = mass (g) of charcoal · C = equilibrium concentration of acetic acid in solution after adsorption · k and n are constants for the acetic acid–charcoal system.

Section 3 — non-negative numerical value (+4 for the correct value, 0 otherwise, no negative marking). There are no printed options. What there are are four decisions on the way to k, and every one of them is a place students lose the mark. The lab settles all four by measurement.

  • Claim A
    0.45 g of acetic acid dissolved in 50 mL of water is 0.15 mol L−1.
  • Claim B
    Adding charcoal removes acetic acid from the flask, so the total amount of acid goes down.
  • Claim C
    pH = 3.0 means the acetic acid left in solution is 1.0 × 10−3 mol L−1.
  • Claim D
    A slope of 1 makes the isotherm x/m = kC, so k is x/m divided by the equilibrium C.

03Bench B — charcoal, and the shaker

Charcoal is riddled with pores, so one gram of it carries an enormous surface. Drop it in, shake the flask, and watch where the acid molecules end up. Keep an eye on the totals.

The flask

Each dot is 0.01 g of acetic acid. The dark grains are charcoal.
What adsorption is. The acid molecules are not reacting and they are not being destroyed — they stick to the outside of the charcoal. That is what the word adsorb means: onto a surface, not into the solid. Everything you put in is still in the flask.

04Bench C — the pH meter, and the mass balance

The meter measures hydrogen ions, not acetic acid. Turning one into the other is the step this question is really about — and once you have it, the mass balance falls out.

Measure the solution

A digital pH meter. The probe reads the hydrogen-ion activity.
Why the meter is not enough. Acetic acid is weak — only about one molecule in a hundred has given its proton away. So the hydrogen ions you measure are a small sample of the acid that is really there. Ka is the conversion factor between the two.

Where the 0.45 g went

The bar always adds up to the 0.45 g you weighed out.

The books

The mass balance fills in once you have the equilibrium concentration.
—x/m — grams of acid held per gram of charcoal
What x/m means. Two grams of charcoal would hold twice as much acid, so the raw mass adsorbed is not a property of the charcoal. Dividing by the mass of charcoal gives a number that describes the material rather than the amount of it.

05Bench D — the Freundlich plot

Take logarithms of the isotherm and it straightens out. The slope of that straight line is the only thing standing between you and k.

Straightening the isotherm

x/m = k C1/n
log10(x/m) = log10k + (1/n) log10C

That is y = c + sx. The slope s is 1/n and the intercept is log10k. Set the slope the question gives you and read the rest off.

07Step-by-step solution

The solution is still locked

Test all four statements on the bench above and the derivation opens by itself.
Statements tested: 0 of 4 — dissolve the acid, shake it with charcoal, measure the pH and read the plot

  1. What went into the flask

    moles = 0.45 g ÷ 60 g mol−1 = 0.0075 mol
    Cinitial = 0.0075 mol ÷ 0.050 L = 0.15 mol L−1

    The volume has to be in litres. Dividing by 50 instead of 0.050 gives 0.00015, which is moles per millilitre.

  2. The pH reading is about H+, not about the acid

    [H+] = 10−pH = 10−3.0 = 1.0 × 10−3 mol L−1

    Acetic acid is weak: only a small fraction of it has given up a proton. So the hydrogen ions you measure are nowhere near the amount of acid present. That conversion is the whole point of being handed Ka.

  3. Ka turns [H+] into the acid concentration

    CH3COOH ⇌ CH3COO− + H+     Ka = [CH3COO−][H+] / [CH3COOH]

    Both ions come only from the acid, so [CH3COO−] ≈ [H+], and the expression collapses:

    Ka = [H+]² / C  ⟹  C = [H+]² / Ka
    C = (1.0 × 10−3)² ÷ (1.0 × 10−5) = 0.10 mol L−1

    Sense check: 0.10 M is below the 0.15 M you started with, which is exactly what adsorption should do.

  4. Mass balance — the acid moved, it did not vanish

    nleft = C × V = 0.10 × 0.050 = 0.005 mol
    mass left = 0.005 × 60 = 0.30 g
    x = 0.45 − 0.30 = 0.15 g adsorbed

    0.30 g in the solution plus 0.15 g on the charcoal is the 0.45 g you weighed out. A third of the acid ended up on the surface.

  5. x/m

    x/m = 0.15 g ÷ 1.0 g = 0.15

    x is a mass, as the question states, so x/m is grams per gram — a pure number.

  6. The slope gives n

    log10(x/m) = log10k + (1/n) log10C

    That is a straight line whose gradient is 1/n. The question hands you the gradient:

    1/n = 1  ⟹  n = 1  ⟹  x/m = kC

  7. k

    0.15 = k × (0.10)1  ⟹  k = 0.15 ÷ 0.10 = 1.5
    units: (g/g) ÷ (mol L−1) = L mol−1

    The C in the isotherm is the concentration left at equilibrium, 0.10 M — not the 0.15 M you started with. Using 0.15 gives 1.0 and is the commonest wrong answer here.

  8. Answer

    k = 1.5 L mol−1

    Marking: +4 for 1.5, 0 otherwise. No negative marking, so there is never a reason to leave this blank.

✓ Independent verification

  • Derived before anything was looked up. Every step — the mole calculation, the Ka inversion, the mass balance, the slope and the final division — was fixed from first principles, and only then compared with a published solution.
  • The Ka route checked in both directions: forwards it gives C = 0.10 M from pH 3.00, and substituting back, [H+] = √(KaC) = 1.000 × 10−3, reproducing the meter reading exactly.
  • Conservation checked explicitly: 0.30 g in solution + 0.15 g adsorbed = 0.45 g weighed out, to machine precision. The simulation enforces the same identity frame by frame while the flask is shaking.
  • Two independent routes to k: dividing (x/m) by C gives 1.5, and reading the intercept of the log–log line gives log10k = 0.176091, i.e. k = 1.500000. They agree.
  • Units confirmed by dimensional analysis: (g g−1) ÷ (mol L−1) = L mol−1, which is the unit the question asks for — a check that x was correctly taken as a mass rather than a number of moles.
  • The traps reproduced deliberately: using the initial 0.15 M gives exactly 1.0; treating pH 3 as the acid concentration gives an absurd 150; dividing by 50 instead of 0.050 L gives 0.00015; putting x in moles gives 0.025 L g−1. None is 1.5.
  • The approximation quantified rather than assumed: solving the weak-acid equilibrium exactly, without treating the remaining acid as wholly undissociated, gives C = 0.1010 M and k = 1.455 — a 3.0% shift. The question's round numbers are chosen so the standard approximation lands on a clean 1.5, which is the intended answer.

08Concepts and classification

Relations used

moles = mass ÷ molar mass
C = moles ÷ volume in litres
[H+] = 10−pH
weak acid: Ka = [H+]² / C ⟹ C = [H+]²/Ka
mass balance: x = mass taken − mass left in solution
Freundlich: x/m = k C1/n
log form: log(x/m) = log k + (1/n) log C
slope = 1/n  ·  intercept = log k
adsorption is a surface process — nothing leaves the flask

Teacher notes

  • Objective. Two chapters in one question: the ionic-equilibrium step that turns a pH into a concentration, and the surface-chemistry step that turns a concentration into k. Students who know both separately still lose this one at the join.
  • Teaching order that works. Ask the class for the acid concentration at pH 3 before mentioning Ka. Most will say 10−3 M. Then hand them Ka and let them find 0.10 M — a hundred times bigger.
  • Expected wrong answers. 1.0 by far (using the initial 0.15 M in the isotherm); then 150 (treating pH as the acid concentration); occasionally 0.025 (putting x in moles).
  • The step that decides it. C in the Freundlich equation is the concentration left at equilibrium. Say it twice.
  • Discussion question. "If we doubled the charcoal to 2.0 g, what would happen to x, to x/m, and to k?" (More acid adsorbed, a lower equilibrium C, but k unchanged — it is a constant of the system.)
  • Board demonstration. Shaking tea with a spoonful of activated charcoal decolourises it in under a minute. Same physics, and it lands.
