06Step-by-step solution

The solution is still locked

Test all four statements on the bench above and the derivation opens by itself.
Statements tested: 0 of 4 — open XeF₄, XeF₆ and XeO₃ on the bench and run the fluorinating assay

  1. Identify P from the stoichiometry, not from the arrow

    Xenon and fluorine give three fluorides and the ratio picks which one. The paper's 1 : 5 mixture at 873 K and 7 bar is the standard preparation of xenon tetrafluoride.

    Xe + 2F2 —873 K, 7 bar—> XeF4   (1 : 5 Xe : F2)

    For contrast: 2 : 1 at 673 K and 1 bar gives XeF2, and 1 : 20 at 573 K under 60–70 bar gives XeF6. P = XeF4.

  2. O2F2 at 143 K pushes P up one fluoride

    Dioxygen difluoride is a violent low-temperature fluorinating agent. It donates two fluorine atoms and leaves as O2.

    XeF4 + O2F2 —143 K—> XeF6 + O2

    Count the fluorine: 4 on the left in XeF4 plus 2 from O2F2 is 6. Xenon goes from +4 to +6. Q = XeF6.

  3. Complete hydrolysis of Q strips every fluorine

    Partial hydrolysis would stop at XeOF4 or XeO2F2. The word complete takes it all the way to the trioxide.

    XeF6 + 3H2O ⟶ XeO3 + 6HF

    Xenon stays at +6 — this is a substitution, not a redox step. R = XeO3.

  4. (A) XeF4 — two lone pairs, square planar

    Xe has 8 valence electrons · 4 go into Xe–F bonds · 8 − 4 = 4 left = 2 lone pairs

    Steric number 6, so the electron geometry is octahedral; the two lone pairs take the axial positions, leaving the four fluorines square planar. (A) is correct.

  5. (B) XeF6 — one lone pair, so it cannot be a perfect octahedron

    8 valence electrons − 6 bonds = 2 left = 1 lone pair · steric number 7

    Seven electron pairs cannot arrange themselves octahedrally. The lone pair caps one triangular face and pushes the three fluorines around it apart, giving a distorted (capped) octahedron with C3v symmetry. A perfect octahedron would need six equivalent bonds and no lone pair. (B) is incorrect.

  6. (C) XeF6 is a fluorinating agent

    Xenon sits at its highest common oxidation state, +6, held there by six fluorines. Anything that will accept fluorine takes it, and the xenon is reduced — usually all the way to the free element.

    XeF6 + 3H2 ⟶ Xe + 6HF   ·   SF4 + XeF6 ⟶ SF6 + XeF4

    All three xenon fluorides fluorinate; the hexafluoride is the strongest of them. (C) is correct.

  7. (D) XeO3 — trigonal pyramidal

    8 valence electrons − (3 × 2 for three Xe=O bonds) = 2 left = 1 lone pair

    Three bond pairs and one lone pair: steric number 4, tetrahedral electron geometry, and the molecule you can see is a pyramid — the same shape as NH3, with the lone pair squeezing the O–Xe–O angle below 109.5° to about 103°. (D) is correct.

  8. Answer

    (A), (C) and (D)

    Marking: +4 only for choosing all three; +3 for any two of them; +2 … −1 for including (B).

✓ Independent verification

  • Shapes from counting, not memory: every geometry on this page is derived by the same routine — valence electrons minus bonding electrons, halved — so XeF4 → 2 lone pairs, XeF6 → 1, XeO3 → 1 come out of one rule applied three times.
  • Atom balance: Xe + 2F2 → XeF4; XeF4 + O2F2 → XeF6 + O2; XeF6 + 3H2O → XeO3 + 6HF. Each balances element by element, checked in the ledger above.
  • Oxidation-state ladder: +4 → +6 → +6. The O2F2 step is the only redox step; hydrolysis is a substitution, which is why R is a xenon(VI) oxide and not xenon metal.
  • Why (B) fails precisely: perfect octahedral needs steric number 6 with zero lone pairs. XeF6 has steric number 7. The bench refuses to place seven pairs octahedrally, which is the geometric statement behind the chemistry.
  • Distractor audit: (B) is believable because XeF6 looks octahedral on paper and is often drawn that way. The trap is the difference between six bonds and seven electron pairs.
  • Literature cross-check: XeF4 square planar (D4h), XeF6 distorted octahedral (fluxional C3v), XeO3 trigonal pyramidal with O–Xe–O ≈ 103°.
