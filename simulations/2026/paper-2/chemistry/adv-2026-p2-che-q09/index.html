07Step-by-step solution

The solution is still locked

Test all four statements on the bench above and the derivation opens by itself.
Statements tested: 0 of 4 — oxidise both compounds, run Fehling's on fructose, hydrolyse sucrose and finish the average

  1. (A) HNO3 oxidises both ends of a hexose

    Nitric acid is a strong oxidising agent. Open-chain glucose offers it two oxidisable positions — the C1 aldehyde and the C6 primary alcohol — and it takes both.

    glucose  —HNO3—>  saccharic acid   (CHO → COOH and CH2OH → COOH)

    Gluconic acid is simply glucose whose C1 has already been oxidised — by a mild oxidant such as bromine water, which stops there. Its C6 −CH2OH is still untouched, so HNO3 takes it to the same dicarboxylic acid.

    gluconic acid  —HNO3—>  saccharic acid   (CH2OH → COOH)

    Both routes land on saccharic acid. The statement's first half is fine; the clause "whereas glucose is not oxidized to saccharic acid" is what breaks it. (A) is incorrect.

  2. (B) Fehling's is alkaline, and alkali rearranges a ketose

    Fructose has no aldehyde of its own. But Fehling's solution is strongly basic, and base removes the acidic α-hydrogen at C1 to give a 1,2-enediol. That enediol can collapse back in three directions.

    fructose ⇌ 1,2-enediol ⇌ glucose  ·  enediol ⇌ mannose

    This is the Lobry de Bruyn–van Ekenstein rearrangement. It produces glucose and mannose — two aldohexoses differing only at C2, which is exactly the "another aldohexose" the statement names. Aldoses reduce Cu(II) to Cu2O, the red precipitate.

    RCHO + 2Cu2+ + 5OH− ⟶ RCOO− + Cu2O↓ + 3H2O

    So a ketose does give a positive test, and for exactly the reason stated. (B) is correct.

  3. (C) Sucrose splits 1 : 1

    C12H22O11 + H2O ⟶ C6H12O6 + C6H12O6   (342.30 + 18.02 = 180.16 + 180.16)

    One glycosidic bond, one water, two monosaccharides — one D-glucose and one D-fructose, in strictly equal numbers. That equimolar mixture is what "invert sugar" names. (C) is correct.

  4. Why it is called invert sugar

    Sucrose is dextrorotatory, [α] = +66.5°. The mixture it hydrolyses into is laevorotatory. The sign of the rotation flips during the reaction — the rotation inverts. It has nothing to do with any configuration inverting.

  5. (D) The number is −20°, not −40°

    Start from the printed data, which is for the L sugars. Enantiomers rotate equally and oppositely, so the D sugars follow by a sign flip:

    L-(−)-glucose −52.5°  ⟹  D-(+)-glucose +52.5°
    L-(+)-fructose +92.5°  ⟹  D-(−)-fructose −92.5°

    Invert sugar is equimolar, and glucose and fructose have the same molar mass (180.16), so equal moles means equal mass and the specific rotation of the mixture is the plain average:

    [α] = (+52.5 − 92.5) / 2 = −20°

    −40° is what you get by adding the two and never dividing. It is the right pair of numbers with the last step missing. (D) is incorrect.

  6. Answer

    (B) and (C)

    Marking: +4 for both; +2 for either one alone; −1 for including (A) or (D).

✓ Independent verification

  • Functional groups counted, not eyeballed: every structure was written as SMILES and matched against SMARTS patterns — glucose returns one aldehyde and one primary alcohol, gluconic acid one carboxyl and one primary alcohol, saccharic acid two carboxyls and no oxidisable end left. That is why both starting materials converge.
  • Isomerisation is atom-conserving: fructose, glucose and mannose all return the formula C6H12O6, confirming the alkaline step rearranges rather than adds or removes anything.
  • Glucose and mannose confirmed as C2 epimers: comparing assigned stereocentres, they differ at exactly one centre — so "another aldohexose" is a real, specific compound and not hand-waving.
  • Hydrolysis mass balance: 342.30 (sucrose) + 18.02 (water) = 360.31 = 180.16 + 180.16. Equal molar masses are also what make the plain average legitimate in (D).
  • The average computed by mass fraction, not assumed: weighting properly by mass fraction gives −20.000°, identical to the simple average, because the two molar masses are equal. The distinction matters whenever they are not.
  • The trap reproduced deliberately: +52.5 + (−92.5) = −40 exactly, confirming that the printed distractor is the sum rather than a different chemistry.

08Concepts and classification

Relations used

mild oxidation (Br2/H2O): CHO → COOH only ⟹ gluconic acid
strong oxidation (HNO3): CHO → COOH and CH2OH → COOH ⟹ saccharic acid
ketose + OH⁻ ⇌ 1,2-enediol ⇌ aldoses (Lobry de Bruyn–van Ekenstein)
RCHO + 2Cu²⁺ + 5OH⁻ ⟶ RCOO⁻ + Cu2O↓ + 3H2O
sucrose + H2O ⟶ D-glucose + D-fructose (1 : 1)
enantiomers: [α]D equal in size, opposite in sign
mixture: [α] = Σ (mass fraction × [α]i)
D / L is configuration · (+) / (−) is observed rotation · the two are independent

Teacher notes

  • Objective. Separate three ideas students routinely fuse: what an oxidant attacks, what alkali does to a ketose, and what "specific rotation of a mixture" means.
  • Teaching order that works. Run bench D first in class — the −40 trap is the fastest way to get attention — then go back to A, B, C.
  • Expected wrong answers. Most students pick (A) and (C), or (B), (C) and (D). The (D) error is arithmetic, not chemical, so it survives a strong conceptual student.
  • Discussion question. "If invert sugar were equal masses instead of equal moles, would the answer change?" (Here no — identical molar masses. Ask them why that matters.)
  • Shortcut worth teaching. Any statement of the form "X is oxidised but Y is not" deserves a check of what oxidisable groups Y still has. Glucose has two.
  • Board demonstration. Fehling's on fructose is a real thirty-second demo and it lands hard — students expect nothing to happen.
