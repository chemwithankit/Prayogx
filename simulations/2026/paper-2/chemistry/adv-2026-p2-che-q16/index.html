Two volatile liquids A and B form an ideal solution. Consider a 5 molal solution of B in A inside a closed container having a total vapour pressure of 100 mm Hg at 300 K. The vapour pressure of pure A at 300 K is 105 mm Hg. Assume that A and B behave as ideal gases in the vapour phase.

Q.16  The mole fraction of B in vapour phase which is in equilibrium with this solution is ____ .

The whole question is in one word: vapour. The liquid's composition, xB, follows from the molality in two lines. The vapour sitting above it has a different composition, yB, because the two liquids do not evaporate equally readily. Writing down xB and stopping is the mistake this question is built to catch.

06Step-by-step solution

Run the experiment and reveal the answer — the derivation opens with it.

The solution is still locked

The bench has not produced an answer yet. Enter the data, run the experiment through to the end and press REVEAL ANSWER — this opens by itself at the same moment.

  1. Step 1 — moles of A

    Molality is quoted per kilogram of solvent, so take 1000 g of A as the basis. Nothing about the answer depends on that choice; it just gives us something to count.

    nA = 1000 / 50 = 20 mol

  2. Step 2 — the liquid-phase mole fraction of B

    A 5 molal solution has 5 mol of B for every kilogram of A, so nB = 5 mol. Mole fraction counts against everything present, not against the solvent alone.

    xA = 20 / (20 + 5) = 0.8  ·  xB = 5 / 25 = 0.2

    This is the number the question is waiting for you to hand in by mistake.

  3. Step 3 — partial pressure of A, from Raoult's law

    In an ideal solution each component exerts its own pure vapour pressure scaled by its own mole fraction in the liquid.

    pA = xA pA° = 0.8 × 105 = 84 mm Hg

  4. Step 4 — partial pressure of B, from the total

    The gauge on the vessel reads the sum of the two partial pressures, and A's share is now known, so B's share is whatever is left.

    pB = pT − pA = 100 − 84 = 16 mm Hg

  5. Step 5 — the vapour-phase mole fraction

    The vapour is an ideal gas mixture, so each component's partial pressure is its mole fraction in the vapour times the total pressure — Dalton's law. Rearranged, the vapour composition is simply the pressure share.

    pi = yi pT  ⟹  yB = pB / pT = 16 / 100 = 0.16

  6. Why it is smaller than xB, and what that means

    Putting the steps together gives yB = xBpB°/pT, and here pB° = 16/0.2 = 80 mm Hg against pA° = 105 mm Hg. B is the less volatile of the two, so the vapour is poorer in B than the liquid was: 0.16 against 0.20, an enrichment factor of exactly pB°/pT = 0.8.

    yB / xB = pB° / pT = 80/100 = 0.8  ·  yA = 84/100 = 0.84 > xA = 0.80

    The vapour is always richer in the more volatile component. That single sentence is the basis of fractional distillation, and it is what this question is really about.

Independent verification

  • Derived first, checked afterwards. Every step — the molality conversion, Raoult, the subtraction and Dalton — was worked out from the stem alone before any published solution was consulted.
  • The two phases close separately. xA + xB = 0.80 + 0.20 = 1 and yA + yB = 0.84 + 0.16 = 1, and the partial pressures add back to the measured total: 84 + 16 = 100 mm Hg.
  • The answer by a second, independent route. Recovering pB° = pB/xB = 80 mm Hg and using yB = xBpB°/pT gives 0.16 again, as does 1 − xApA°/pT. The relative-volatility form with α = pA°/pB° = 21/16 reproduces yA = 0.84 exactly.
  • The sign of the effect predicted before it was computed. pB° < pA° forces yB < xB, and the computed numbers agree — so the result is not a coincidence of arithmetic.
  • Limiting cases. Setting pA° = pT makes the two liquids equally volatile and the model returns yB = xB exactly; setting pA° below pT makes B the more volatile one and the model returns yB > xB; a vanishing molality returns yB → 0. The model therefore behaves correctly across its whole range, not only at the paper's numbers.
  • The basis cancels. Repeating the calculation with 500 g, 2500 g or 137 g of A returns 0.16 every time, which the bench reproduces live — the mass of A moves n(A) and n(B) on the gauges and never moves the answer.
  • Distractor audit. Handing in xB gives 0.20; yA gives 0.84; pB/pA gives 0.1905; xBpA°/pT gives 0.21; 1 − xB gives 0.80. None is 0.16, and every one is reproduced on the bench as a run.
  • Headless-browser run of this page. The finished simulation was driven end to end in Chromium and asserted to reproduce 0.16 from the live model, to give different answers on different data, to refuse a total pressure that would make pB negative, to keep the answer off the page until it is revealed, and to render with no console errors and no horizontal overflow at 390 px and 360 px.
  • Cross-check. Afterwards the answer was compared against a published worked solution for this paper, which follows the same route and reports the same intermediate values (xB = 0.2, pA = 84, pB = 16 mm Hg) and the same 0.16. IIT Roorkee's official key was not retrievable at the time of writing.

07What did we learn?

Four ideas, in the order the bench used them. The cards fill their numbers in when the answer is revealed.

The four concepts this question turns on

CARD 01

Molality ≠ mole fraction

5 molal does not mean xB = 5, and it does not mean 5/20 either. Molality counts moles of B per kilogram of A; mole fraction counts them against every mole in the liquid. Converting between them is what the molar mass of A is given for.

xB = nB / (nA + nB)  ⟹  …
CARD 02

Raoult's law — the liquid's side

Each component of an ideal solution pushes molecules into the vapour in proportion to how much of the liquid it owns, scaled by how volatile it is on its own.

pi = xi pi°  ⟹  …
CARD 03

Dalton's law — the vapour's side

Once the molecules are in the gas phase they are an ideal gas mixture, and there each component's partial pressure is its own mole fraction times the total. This is the step that turns pressures back into a composition.

pi = yi pT  ⟹  …
CARD 04

The key connection

Put the two laws together and the vapour composition is the liquid composition scaled by that component's own volatility relative to the total pressure. Less volatile than the mixture as a whole means the vapour is poorer in it; more volatile means richer.

yi = xi pi° / pT  ⟹  …

Formulas used

nA = wA/MA,   nB = m · wA/1000
xB = nB/(nA + nB),   xA = 1 − xB
pA = xA pA°  (Raoult)
pB = pT − pA
yB = pB / pT  (Dalton)
combined: yB = xB pB° / pT,   yB/xB = pB°/pT

Everything here is a ratio of pressures or of moles, so the answer is dimensionless — and the gas constant, the molar mass of B and the density of B, all printed in the stem, never appear. They belong to Q.15.
