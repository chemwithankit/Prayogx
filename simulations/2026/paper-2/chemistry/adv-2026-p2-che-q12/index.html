01The question — verbatim

Section 3, numerical value — +4 for the correct value, 0 otherwise.

In a solvent S, a compound B is partially dissociated into C and D as given below:

B ⇌ 2C + 2D

B, C and D are non-volatile in nature. The molar mass of B is 10 times the molar mass of S. The standard boiling point and the standard enthalpy of vaporization of S are 400 K and 10R J mol−1, respectively (R is the gas constant in J K−1 mol−1). A solution of B in S with an initial concentration of B as 0.25% (mass/mass) has a boiling point of 408 K at 1 bar pressure. In this solution, the mole percent of B that has been dissociated is ______.

Not given: the molar mass of the solvent itself. That is deliberate — part of the experiment below is finding out why it is not needed.

04Where it goes wrong

Four answers that come out of this question every year. Each one is a single wrong move, and the bench above will reproduce all of them if you make it.

100 — using i = 1 + α

Reading “B ⇌ 2C + 2D” as though one molecule became one particle. One B gives four particles, so i = 1 − α + 4α = 1 + 3α. With i = 1 + α you get α = 1, i.e. 100% — complete dissociation, which the word partially in the question rules out.

404 K — forgetting dissociation

Taking i = 1 gives ΔTb = 4 K and a boiling point of 404 K. The thermometer says 408 K, so the extra 4 K has to come from somewhere — and the only thing left is more particles.

102 — using Tb instead of ΔTb

Putting 408 K into ΔTb = i Kb m gives i = 102. The van’t Hoff factor here can never exceed 4, so any i above 4 is a signal that a boiling point was used where a rise in boiling point was meant.

stuck — looking for MS

The molar mass of the solvent is not in the question and it is not missing. Kb carries one factor of MS and the molality carries one factor of 1/MS; the product is 4 K whatever the solvent is. The second dial on the bench demonstrates it.

06Step-by-step solution

Match the thermometer to 408.00 K on the bench and the derivation opens by itself.

The solution is still locked

Turn the dissociation dial until the thermometer reads 408.00 K — the boiling point the question quotes — and both this section and the answer box unlock.

  1. Build Kb out of the solvent’s own data

    No table of ebullioscopic constants is given, and none is needed. Kb follows from the boiling point and the enthalpy of vaporisation:

    Kb = R (Tb°)2 MS / ΔHvap

    with MS in kg mol−1. Because ΔHvap is quoted as 10R, the gas constant cancels straight away:

    Kb = R(400)2(MS/1000) / (10R) = 16 MS   K kg mol−1   (MS in g mol−1)

  2. Turn 0.25% (m/m) into a molality

    0.25 g of B in 100 g of solvent, and MB = 10 MS:

    nB = 0.25 / (10 MS) mol   in   0.100 kg of S
    m = 0.25 / (10 MS × 0.100) = 0.25 / MS   mol kg−1

  3. The molar mass of the solvent cancels

    This is the move the question is really testing. Kb goes up with MS, the molality goes down with it, and the product is a pure number:

    Kb m = (16 MS) × (0.25 / MS) = 4.000 K

    So the solvent’s identity never enters the answer, which is why it was never given.

  4. Read the van’t Hoff factor off the thermometer

    ΔTb = Tb − Tb° = 408 − 400 = 8 K
    i = ΔTb / (Kb m) = 8 / 4 = 2

    Two particles now exist for every one that was dissolved. An undissociated solution would have boiled at 404 K, so the second 4 K is entirely the work of the dissociation.

  5. Count the particles the dissociation makes

    Start with 1 mol of B and let a fraction α break up. Each one that goes produces 2 C and 2 D — four particles from one:

    B ⇌ 2C + 2D   →   (1 − α) + 2α + 2α = 1 + 3α

    The limits check out: α = 0 gives i = 1, and complete dissociation gives i = 4.

  6. Solve for α

    1 + 3α = 2   ⇒   α = 1/3 = 0.3333
    mole percent dissociated = 100 α = 33.33 %

    Which is exactly the reading the bench locks onto: 4 of the 12 drawn units broken up, 8 whole B left plus 16 fragments — 24 particles where there were 12.

Independent verification

  • Derived before anything was looked up. Kb from thermodynamics, the molality from the mass percent, i from the measured rise and α from the stoichiometry — all fixed independently, and only then compared with a published solution.
  • Back-substitution. Taking any MS at all — 12, 20, 33.3, 58.5, 180 g mol−1 — and putting α = 1/3 back into ΔTb = i Kb m returns 8.000000 K and a boiling point of exactly 408 K.
  • The cancellation checked symbolically, not just numerically: Kb m reduces to the constant 4 with MS carried as a free symbol, so the result holds for every solvent, not merely for the values tried.
  • Limiting cases. i = 1 + 3α gives i = 1 at α = 0 and i = 4 at α = 1, bracketing boiling points of 404 K and 416 K. The measured 408 K sits inside that window, so a partially dissociated solution is consistent with the data — as the question states.
  • The distractors reproduced deliberately. i = 1 + α gives 100%; ignoring dissociation gives 404 K; using Tb in place of ΔTb gives i = 102, above the maximum of 4; ignoring the factor of ten in MB multiplies the molality by ten and gives a rise of 80 K. None of them is 33.33.
  • Units. Kb in K kg mol−1 times m in mol kg−1 leaves kelvin, and i is dimensionless — so ΔTb comes out in kelvin as it must.
  • Answer key cross-check. A published worked solution for this paper follows the same route — Kb = 16 MS, Kbm = 4, i = 2, i = 1 + 3α — and reports the same 33.33. IIT Roorkee’s official key was not retrievable at the time of writing.
