01The question — verbatim

Section 3, numerical value — +4 for the correct value, 0 otherwise. Read it, then type what it gives you into the bench below.

In a solvent S, a compound B is partially dissociated into C and D as given below:

B ⇌ 2C + 2D

B, C and D are non-volatile in nature. The molar mass of B is 10 times the molar mass of S. The standard boiling point and the standard enthalpy of vaporization of S are 400 K and 10R J mol−1, respectively (R is the gas constant in J K−1 mol−1). A solution of B in S with an initial concentration of B as 0.25% (mass/mass) has a boiling point of 408 K at 1 bar pressure. In this solution, the mole percent of B that has been dissociated is ______.

Not given: the molar mass of the solvent itself. That is deliberate — section 03 shows why the experiment never needs it.

04Where it goes wrong

Four answers this question produces every year. Each is a single wrong move, and the bench above will reproduce all of them if you enter the data that way.

100 — one particle instead of four

Reading “B ⇌ 2C + 2D” as though one molecule became one. Enter 1 C and 1 D in the stoichiometry boxes and the bench returns 100 % — complete dissociation, which the word partially rules out. One B leaves four particles, so i = 1 + 3α.

404 K — forgetting dissociation

Step 3 of the run boils the solution with nothing dissociated and lands on 404 K. The thermometer says 408 K, so the extra 4 K has to come from somewhere — and the only thing left is more particles.

102 — Tb instead of ΔTb

Putting 408 K into ΔTb = i Kb m gives i = 102. The van’t Hoff factor here can never exceed 4, so any i above 4 says a boiling point was used where a rise was meant. Enter 808 K as the solution’s boiling point and the bench refuses the run for exactly that reason.

stuck — hunting for MS

The molar mass of the solvent is not in the question and it is not missing. There is no input box for it on the bench because the experiment does not need one — section 03 shows why.

06Step-by-step solution

Complete a run on the bench and the derivation opens by itself.

The solution is still locked

Enter the data from the question and let the experiment finish — the derivation and the answer box unlock the moment the flask matches your measurement.

  1. Build Kb out of the solvent’s own data

    No table of ebullioscopic constants is given, and none is needed. Kb follows from the boiling point and the enthalpy of vaporisation:

    Kb = R (Tb°)2 MS / ΔHvap

    with MS in kg mol−1. Because ΔHvap is quoted as 10R, the gas constant cancels straight away:

    Kb = R(400)2(MS/1000) / (10R) = 16 MS   K kg mol−1   (MS in g mol−1)

  2. Turn 0.25% (m/m) into a molality

    0.25 g of B in 100 g of solvent, and MB = 10 MS:

    nB = 0.25 / (10 MS) mol   in   0.100 kg of S
    m = 0.25 / (10 MS × 0.100) = 0.25 / MS   mol kg−1

  3. The molar mass of the solvent cancels

    This is the move the question is really testing. Kb goes up with MS, the molality goes down with it, and the product is a pure number:

    Kb m = (16 MS) × (0.25 / MS) = 4.000 K

    So the solvent’s identity never enters the answer, which is why it was never given and why the bench has no input box for it.

  4. Read the van’t Hoff factor off the thermometer

    ΔTb = Tb − Tb° = 408 − 400 = 8 K
    i = ΔTb / (Kb m) = 8 / 4 = 2

    Two particles now exist for every one that was dissolved. That is exactly what step 4 of the run exposes: an undissociated solution boils at 404 K, and the second 4 K is entirely the work of the dissociation.

  5. Count the particles the dissociation makes

    Start with 1 mol of B and let a fraction α break up. Each one that goes produces 2 C and 2 D — four particles from one:

    B ⇌ 2C + 2D   →   (1 − α) + 2α + 2α = 1 + 3α

    The limits check out: α = 0 gives i = 1, and complete dissociation gives i = 4.

  6. Solve for α

    1 + 3α = 2   ⇒   α = 1/3 = 0.3333
    mole percent dissociated = 100 α = 33.33 %

    Which is what the flask shows at the end of the run: 4 of the 12 drawn units broken up, 8 whole B plus 16 fragments — 24 particles where there were 12.

Independent verification

  • Derived before anything was looked up. Kb from thermodynamics, the molality from the mass percent, i from the measured rise and α from the stoichiometry — all fixed independently, and only then compared with a published solution.
  • Back-substitution. Taking any MS at all — 12, 20, 33.3, 58.5, 180 g mol−1 — and putting α = 1/3 back into ΔTb = i Kb m returns 8.000000 K and a boiling point of exactly 408 K.
  • The cancellation checked symbolically, not just numerically: Kb m reduces to the constant 4 with MS carried as a free symbol, so the result holds for every solvent, not merely for the values tried. The bench proves the same thing by construction — it never asks for MS and still reaches the answer.
  • Limiting cases. i = 1 + 3α gives i = 1 at α = 0 and i = 4 at α = 1, bracketing boiling points of 404 K and 416 K. The measured 408 K sits inside that window, so a partially dissociated solution is consistent with the data — as the question states. The bench enforces the same bracket and refuses any measurement outside it, saying why.
  • The distractors reproduced deliberately. One C and one D in the stoichiometry boxes gives 100 %; ignoring dissociation gives 404 K; using Tb in place of ΔTb gives i = 102, above the maximum of 4; ignoring the factor of ten in MB multiplies the molality by ten and gives a rise of 80 K. None of them is 33.33.
  • Units. Kb in K kg mol−1 times m in mol kg−1 leaves kelvin, and i is dimensionless — so ΔTb comes out in kelvin as it must.
  • Answer key cross-check. A published worked solution for this paper follows the same route — Kb = 16 MS, Kbm = 4, i = 2, i = 1 + 3α — and reports the same 33.33. IIT Roorkee’s official key was not retrievable at the time of writing.
