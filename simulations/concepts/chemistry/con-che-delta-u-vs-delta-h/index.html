Two Calorimeters, One Reaction — why a sealed bomb measures ΔU and an open vessel measures ΔH

NCERT Class 11 · Chemistry Part I · Ch 5 Thermodynamics · §5.2.2(a) and §5.3 Calorimetry · concept experience

01The question

The same fuel burns in two identical calorimeters, one sealed and one at constant pressure. Why can they report different heats for the same reaction, and when do they agree?

03Detailed solution

  1. Two identical calorimeters. Each is a steel vessel in a stirred, insulated water bath, as in NCERT's bomb calorimeter (Fig. 5.7). The heat capacity of the whole calorimeter is known, Ccal = kJ K⁻¹, the value NCERT uses (NCERT writes it as CV in Problem 5.6). The only difference is the lid: bolted (constant volume) or a free piston at 1 bar (constant pressure).
  2. Heat from the thermometer. The calorimeter gains the heat the reaction loses: qreaction = −Ccal ΔT A rising temperature therefore means q < 0: the reaction is exothermic and ΔrH < 0 (IUPAC signs: energy into the system is positive).
  3. Sealed bomb: no work. The bolted lid cannot move, so ΔV = 0 and w = −pΔV = 0. The first law, ΔU = q + w, gives qV = ΔU
  4. Piston: the gas changes its amount, so the volume changes. At constant pressure and temperature the ideal-gas law gives pΔV = ΔngRT, where Δng = moles of gaseous products − moles of gaseous reactants (solids and liquids do not count). Work crosses the boundary: w = −pΔV = −ΔngRT (per mole of reaction) When gas disappears (Δng < 0) the piston sinks and the atmosphere does work on the system (w > 0), so more heat must leave for the same ΔU.
  5. Heat at constant pressure is ΔH. With H = U + pV: qp = ΔU − w = ΔU + pΔV = ΔH  ⇒  ΔH − ΔU = ΔngRT This is NCERT's eq. 5.10. Here RT = kJ mol⁻¹ (R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, T = 298 K).
  6. The three reactions (ΔrH from NCERT pp. 150–152; ΔrU = ΔrH − ΔngRT; every number below is computed by the page's own model):

  7. When do the two calorimeters agree? Only when Δng = 0. Burning graphite, C(s) + O₂(g) → CO₂(g), uses one mole of gas and makes one, so ΔH = ΔU: a bomb then gives ΔH directly. That is why NCERT's Problem 5.6 can find ΔH from a bomb calorimeter. For gases appearing or disappearing, the bomb gives ΔU, and ΔH follows from the equation above.

Independent checks (computed now by the page)

    Concept. A calorimeter measures heat. Heat equals ΔU only when no work is done (fixed volume), and equals ΔH when the pressure is fixed. The two differ by the expansion work, ΔngRT, which depends only on how many moles of gas the reaction makes or uses.

    Key takeaways

    • Bomb calorimeter (constant volume, no work): qV = ΔU.
    • Constant-pressure calorimeter: qp = ΔH.
    • ΔH − ΔU = ΔngRT; count only gaseous moles.
    • Δng = 0 ⇒ ΔH = ΔU; for combustion the gap is a few kJ mol⁻¹, small but real.
    • A thermometer that rises means qreaction < 0 (exothermic).

    Notes on the source. The right-hand calorimeter is an idealisation: NCERT's constant-pressure calorimeter (Fig. 5.8, a foamed polystyrene cup) is used for reactions in solution, and a fuel cannot be burnt in it; a free piston keeps the comparison fair. The calorimeter, water and stirrer are treated as one known heat capacity, and ΔrU is taken at 298 K (the rise is about 1.5 K). NCERT's Problem 5.6 shows the method with illustrative readings (a 1 K rise for 1 g of graphite), which give about −2.48 × 10² kJ mol⁻¹; the same reaction is listed at −393.5 kJ mol⁻¹ on pp. 151–152, and this experiment uses the listed value. NCERT also writes that ΔU as "kJ K⁻¹"; it is kJ.
