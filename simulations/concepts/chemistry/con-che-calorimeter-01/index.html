How Does a Calorimeter Work?

A reaction gives out heat. Heat cannot be seen. How can we find out how much heat the reaction gave out?

03What the experiment showed

  1. Heat goes into the water. The reaction gives out energy as heat. The water around the reaction tube takes it in and gets warmer.
  2. The thermometer reads temperature, not heat. We use the change in temperature: ΔT = final − initial = 27.5 °C − 25.0 °C = 2.5 °C
  3. The amount of water matters. The same reaction warmed 100 g of water by 5.0 °C, 200 g by 2.5 °C and 400 g by 1.25 °C. More water needs more energy for the same rise.
  4. The rule. The heat taken in by the water is q = m c ΔT where m is the mass of water, c its specific heat capacity (the energy 1 g needs to warm by 1 °C; 4.2 J for water, the value NCERT uses) and ΔT the rise. For the first run: q = 200 × 4.2 × 2.5 = 2100 J = 2.1 kJ. That is the heat the reaction gave out.
  5. Heat loss spoils the measurement. In a poorly insulated cup the same reaction gave only 1.75 °C, so q = m c ΔT would give 1470 J instead of 2100 J. A calorimeter controls heat loss with insulation and a lid.
  6. Bomb calorimeter. Fuels burn in oxygen inside a strong sealed steel vessel, the bomb, which sits in water. Its walls cannot move: ΔV = 0, so no expansion work is done (w = 0) and all of the energy change appears as heat: qV = ΔU
  7. Signs. Heat is counted from the point of view of whatever it belongs to. The water takes in heat, so for the water q is positive. The reaction gives out that heat, so for the reaction q is negative. In the bomb: ΔU = qV = −qwater which is negative for a reaction that gives out heat: the substances lose energy.
  8. Constant pressure. When the boundary can move (a piston under a fixed weight, or the open air), gas made by the reaction expands and does work. With the cylinder in a stirred water bath, the bath warms less than it would if the same reaction were sealed in a rigid container, because some energy lifted the weight. The heat measured at constant pressure is the enthalpy change: qp = ΔH = −qwater also negative for a reaction that gives out heat, but less negative than ΔU when gas is made.
  9. Why ΔH and ΔU can differ. The difference is the expansion work, set by the change in the number of moles of gas: ΔH − ΔU = ΔngRT If no gas moles are made or used up (Δng = 0), ΔH = ΔU.
In one sentence. A calorimeter is an apparatus that finds the heat released or absorbed by a process by observing a temperature change under controlled conditions.

Key takeaways

  • A calorimeter measures heat indirectly, from a temperature change of a known mass of water.
  • Heat taken in by water: q = m c ΔT.
  • Insulation and a lid keep the heat in, so the measurement is fair.
  • Sealed, rigid (constant volume): qV = ΔU. Movable boundary (constant pressure): qp = ΔH. For a reaction that gives out heat, both are negative: the water's heat is +, the reaction's is −.

How to use this lab

  1. Read the green NEXT box in the lab guide, then press the big button (or tap the glowing item on the bench).
  2. Watch the apparatus. When it settles, the guide tells you what you did, what happened and why.
  3. Some scenes have choices: parts to inspect, amounts of water, reactions. Try as many as you like.
  4. Replay repeats the last thing you did, Pause freezes it, Back returns to the previous scene and Reset empties the bench.
  5. Drag sideways across the lab to look around the apparatus.
  6. Classroom fills the screen for a projector. The last scene leads to XP-09, where real fuels are measured.
