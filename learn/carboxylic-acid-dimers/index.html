Why Carboxylic Acids Boil So High: Hydrogen-Bonded Dimers

Video not playing? YouTube

Four molecules of about the same mass boil from 0 °C to 118 °C. Carboxylic acids win because two hydrogen bonds join them into dimers that survive even in the vapour; the same dimers double acetic acid's measured molar mass in benzene.

Key points

  • Similar mass (≈ 60), very different b.p.: butane ≈ 0 °C, propanal 49 °C, propan-1-ol 97 °C, acetic acid 118 °C.
  • The O–H of one acid bonds to the C=O oxygen of another, and back: two hydrogen bonds make a cyclic dimer.
  • The association is stronger and more extensive than in alcohols, and the dimers survive even in the vapour.
  • In benzene, acetic acid's measured molar mass is about 120, double the true 60 (abnormal molar mass).
  • Acids up to C4 are miscible with water; solubility falls as the chain grows.

The dimer

Two acid molecules face each other: each O–H hydrogen bonds to the other molecule's C=O oxygen (O–H···O=C, twice), closing an eight-membered ring. Breaking this association needs more energy, so the boiling point is high; most acids remain dimers even in the vapour phase and in aprotic solvents such as benzene.

Solubility

  • Up to four carbons: miscible with water (hydrogen bonds with water).
  • Longer chains: the hydrocarbon part grows, solubility falls.
  • Benzoic acid: nearly insoluble in cold water.
