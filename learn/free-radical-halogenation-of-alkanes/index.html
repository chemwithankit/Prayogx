Free Radical Mechanism: Halogenation of Alkanes

Video not playing? YouTube

Butane + Cl₂ in UV light gives 1-chlorobutane and 2-chlorobutane, because butane has primary and secondary hydrogens. The free-radical chain has three stages: initiation (Cl₂ splits homolytically into 2 Cl•), propagation (Cl• abstracts an H to give HCl and a butyl radical, which takes Cl from Cl₂ and releases a new Cl•) and termination (two radicals combine). The yield of any one product is low: isomers and polychloro products form a mixture that is hard to separate.

Key points

  • C₄H₁₀ + Cl₂ —UV→ 1-chlorobutane + 2-chlorobutane: one H is replaced by Cl.
  • Butane has 6 primary H (terminal C) and 4 secondary H (middle C), so two products form.
  • Initiation: Cl₂ —UV→ 2 Cl• by homolytic cleavage: each Cl keeps one electron.
  • Propagation 1: Cl• + C₄H₁₀ → HCl + C₄H₉• (hydrogen abstraction; one C–H electron stays on carbon).
  • Propagation 2: C₄H₉• + Cl₂ → C₄H₉Cl + Cl•; the new Cl• keeps the chain going.
  • Termination: two radicals combine, e.g. Cl• + Cl• → Cl₂; the chain stops.
  • Low yield: isomeric and polychloro products form a mixture that is hard to separate.

The overall reaction

C₄H₁₀ + Cl₂ —UV light→ CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂Cl + CH₃CH₂CHClCH₃

One hydrogen atom is replaced by a chlorine atom. Butane's hydrogens sit in two environments: primary (6, on the terminal carbons) and secondary (4, on the middle carbons). Replacing a primary H gives 1-chlorobutane; replacing a secondary H gives 2-chlorobutane.

Stage 1 · Chain initiation

Cl₂ —UV light→ 2 Cl•

UV light breaks the Cl–Cl bond homolytically: the two electrons of the bond split equally, one to each chlorine atom, giving two chlorine free radicals (an atom with an unpaired electron).

Stage 2 · Chain propagation

Step 1, hydrogen abstraction. Cl• pulls an H atom off butane. One electron of the C–H bond pairs with chlorine's electron to make H–Cl; the other electron stays on carbon, giving the butyl free radical.

Cl• + CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₃ → HCl + CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂•

Step 2, product formation. The butyl radical's unpaired electron pairs with one electron of Cl₂ to form the C–Cl bond; the other electron leaves with the second chlorine as a new Cl•.

C₄H₉• + Cl₂ → C₄H₉Cl + Cl•

The new Cl• attacks another butane molecule, so the steps repeat: a chain reaction. Abstraction from a middle carbon gives CH₃CH₂ĊHCH₃ and, by the same steps, 2-chlorobutane.

Stage 3 · Chain termination

Cl• + Cl• → Cl₂

Two radicals combine into a stable molecule (each gives one electron to the new bond). As radicals are used up, the chain reaction stops.

Why the yield is low

  • Different hydrogens (1° and 2°) are replaced, so isomeric products form.
  • The products still have hydrogens, so substitution continues to di- and polychloro compounds.
  • The mixture is hard to separate, so the yield of any single compound is low.

More from Haloalkanes and Haloarenes

Haloalkanes from Alcohols: HX, PCl₃, PCl₅ and SOCl₂One rule: R–OH → R–X, the OH group is replaced by a halogen; only the reagent and its by-products change. HX gives water (anhydrous ZnCl₂ for 1° and 2° alcohols; reactivity 3° > 2° > 1°), PCl₃ gives H₃PO₃ (1 PCl₃ : 3 R–OH), PCl₅ gives POCl₃ + HCl, and SOCl₂ gives SO₂ + HCl, two gases that escape, so the alkyl chloride is pure. Phenol does not react this way: its C–O bond has partial double-bond character.ChemistryThe Polar C–X Bond: Polarity, Bond Length and StrengthThe C–X bond is polar: the halogen pulls the shared pair, so carbon is δ+ and the halogen δ−, and nucleophiles attack that carbon. From F to I the halogen gets bigger, so the C–X bond gets longer (139 → 214 pm) and weaker (452 → 234 kJ mol⁻¹). That is why R–I reacts fastest and R–F slowest, even though fluorine is the most electronegative halogen; and why CH₃Cl, not CH₃F, has the largest dipole moment (μ = q × d).ChemistryOne Atom, Many Lives: Haloalkanes and HaloarenesIn 1847 James Simpson found that chloroform, CHCl₃, could put people to sleep for surgery. Replace a hydrogen of a hydrocarbon with a halogen and you get a haloalkane (halogen on an sp³ alkyl carbon) or a haloarene (halogen on an sp² ring carbon). They are everywhere: chloramphenicol for typhoid, thyroxine with four iodine atoms, chloroquine for malaria, halothane for anaesthesia. The polar C–X bond, with a δ+ carbon, is why they react, and it opens the whole chapter.Chemistry · 9:16 + 16:9Count the Halogens: Mono, Di, Poly, Gem and VicHalogen compounds are classified first by the number of halogen atoms: one is mono, two is di, three or more is poly (tri-, tetra-). Count the halogens, not the carbons: chloroform, CHCl₃, has one carbon but three chlorines, so it is a polyhalogen compound. Dihalides split by position: both halogens on the same carbon is a gem-dihalide (alkylidene halide, e.g. ethylidene chloride, 1,1-dichloroethane); on adjacent carbons a vic-dihalide (alkylene dihalide, e.g. ethylene dichloride, 1,2-dichloroethane).ChemistryAlkyl, Vinyl, Allyl and Aryl Halides: Which Carbon Holds X?Alkyl, vinyl (vinylic), allyl (allylic) and aryl halides differ only in the carbon that holds the halogen. Alkyl: a saturated sp³ carbon (1°, 2°, 3° by the alkyl groups on it). Vinyl: X directly on a carbon of a C=C (sp²). Allyl: X on the sp³ carbon next to a C=C. Aryl: X directly on a carbon of an aromatic ring (sp²), e.g. chlorobenzene. Bonus: X on the sp³ CH₂ next to a benzene ring is a benzyl (benzylic) halide. Identify the carbon attached to X: that's the key.ChemistryNaming Haloalkanes and Haloarenes: Common and IUPAC NamesEvery halide has two correct names. The common name is the alkyl group + halide (isopropyl chloride, ethyl chloride). The IUPAC name writes the halogen as a prefix (fluoro, chloro, bromo, iodo) on the longest chain, numbered from the end that gives the lowest locants; when both ends tie, alphabetical order decides (2-bromo-3-methylbutane). On benzene one halogen keeps the same name (chlorobenzene); two use o-, m-, p- in common names and 1,2 / 1,3 / 1,4 in IUPAC.ChemistryAddition of Halogens to Alkenes: the Bromine TestEthene + Br₂ in CCl₄ gives BrCH₂–CH₂Br: the π bond breaks and one Br adds to each double-bond carbon, giving a vic-dibromide. The reddish-brown colour of bromine disappears, so the reaction is the lab test for unsaturation. Propene gives 1,2-dibromopropane and cyclohexene gives 1,2-dibromocyclohexane. Benzene fails the test: adding Br₂ would destroy its aromatic stability, so the colour stays.ChemistryNCERT Example 6.3: Monochloro Structural Isomers of 2-MethylbutaneFree radical monochlorination of (CH₃)₂CHCH₂CH₃ (2-methylbutane) gives 4 monochloro structural isomers, not 5: the two CH₃ groups on the CH carbon are equivalent, so the molecule has only 4 kinds of hydrogen. Replacing one H of each kind gives 1-chloro-3-methylbutane, 2-chloro-3-methylbutane, 2-chloro-2-methylbutane and 1-chloro-2-methylbutane. Counting stereoisomers (JEE), the total is 6.ChemistryHalogen Exchange: Finkelstein and Swarts ReactionsHalogen exchange (NCERT 6.4.3) swaps the Cl or Br of an alkyl halide for another halogen. Finkelstein: R–X + NaI in dry acetone gives R–I; NaCl / NaBr is insoluble in dry acetone and precipitates, so by Le Chatelier's principle the reaction goes forward. Swarts: heating R–Cl / R–Br with a heavy metallic fluoride (AgF, Hg₂F₂, CoF₂ or SbF₃) gives the alkyl fluoride. Trick: Finkelstein → iodide, Swarts → fluoride.ChemistryName and Classify Haloalkanes: NCERT Questions SolvedThree NCERT exercise questions solved with one checklist: longest chain (look inside the branches), lowest locants, alphabetical order on a tie; and the class (1°, 2°, 3°) is decided only by the carbon that carries the halogen.
