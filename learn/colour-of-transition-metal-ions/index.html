The Light Thief: Why Transition-Metal Ions Are Coloured

Video not playing? YouTube

Ligands split the five d orbitals of a transition-metal ion into a lower and an upper level. A d electron absorbs the one band of visible light whose energy equals the gap and jumps up (a d–d transition); the rest of the light reaches the eye, so we see the complementary colour: Ti³⁺(aq) eats green-yellow and looks purple, Cu²⁺(aq) eats red-orange and looks blue. The ligand sets the gap (ruby and emerald are both Cr³⁺), and d⁰ / d¹⁰ ions such as Sc³⁺ and Zn²⁺ are colourless.

Key points

  • Ligands split the five d orbitals into a lower and an upper level with a small energy gap.
  • A d electron absorbs light whose energy equals the gap and jumps up: a d–d transition, usually in the visible region.
  • We see the complementary colour of the light absorbed: Ti³⁺ absorbs green-yellow → purple; Cu²⁺ absorbs red-orange → blue.
  • The ligand sets the gap (same Cr³⁺: ruby red, emerald green); d⁰ and d¹⁰ ions (Sc³⁺, Ti⁴⁺, Zn²⁺) are colourless.

Worked example

Ti³⁺ (3d¹) in water absorbs light near 500 nm (green-yellow) as its one d electron jumps to the upper level; red and blue are transmitted, so [Ti(H₂O)₆]³⁺ is purple. Ti⁴⁺ (3d⁰) has no d electron and is colourless.

Transition metals are good catalysts because they can adopt multiple oxidation states and form complexes. On a metal surface (iron in the Haber process, V₂O₅ in the Contact process, nickel in hydrogenation) reactant molecules bond to surface atoms using 3d and 4s electrons, so their concentration rises and their bonds weaken. Chromate and dichromate interconvert with pH while Cr stays +6. In acid, Cr₂O₇²⁻ + 14H⁺ + 6e⁻ → 2Cr³⁺ + 7H₂O (E° = 1.33 V): a strong oxidising agent that turns orange to green, oxidising Fe²⁺, I⁻, H₂S and, in the 1954 breathalyser, ethanol.ChemistryThe Mineral Chameleon: Potassium PermanganatePotassium permanganate is made by fusing pyrolusite (MnO₂) with KOH and an oxidiser to give dark green potassium manganate (Mn +6). In neutral or acidic water manganate disproportionates (3MnO₄²⁻ + 4H⁺ → 2MnO₄⁻ + MnO₂ + 2H₂O), the 'mineral chameleon' Glauber saw; commercially it is oxidised at an anode. In acid MnO₄⁻ takes 5 electrons to give almost colourless Mn²⁺ (so it is its own indicator in titrations); in neutral or faintly alkaline solution it takes 3 electrons and gives brown MnO₂.ChemistryNo d Electrons, Still Purple: Why KMnO₄ Is So Intensely ColouredIn MnO₄⁻ manganese is +7 (3d⁰), so no d–d transition is possible. The colour comes from ligand-to-metal charge transfer: visible light moves an electron from an oxygen into the empty d orbitals of Mn. This absorbs green-yellow light (≈ 525 nm), so the solution looks purple. Charge transfer is an allowed transition, so it absorbs far more light than restricted d–d transitions: KMnO₄ is intensely coloured while Ti³⁺(aq) or Mn²⁺(aq) are pale. Chromate (yellow) and dichromate (orange), also d⁰, are coloured the same way.Chemistry
