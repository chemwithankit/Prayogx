Haloalkanes from Alcohols: HX, PCl₃, PCl₅ and SOCl₂

Video not playing? YouTube

One rule: R–OH → R–X, the OH group is replaced by a halogen; only the reagent and its by-products change. HX gives water (anhydrous ZnCl₂ for 1° and 2° alcohols; reactivity 3° > 2° > 1°), PCl₃ gives H₃PO₃ (1 PCl₃ : 3 R–OH), PCl₅ gives POCl₃ + HCl, and SOCl₂ gives SO₂ + HCl, two gases that escape, so the alkyl chloride is pure. Phenol does not react this way: its C–O bond has partial double-bond character.

Key points

  • Rule: R–OH → R–X. The OH group leaves and a halogen takes its place.
  • HX: R–OH + HCl → R–Cl + H₂O; anhydrous ZnCl₂ for 1° and 2°; 3° just shaken with conc. HCl. Reactivity 3° > 2° > 1°.
  • R–Br: NaBr + H₂SO₄ (HBr); R–I: heat with NaI or KI in 95% H₃PO₄.
  • PCl₃: 3 R–OH + PCl₃ → 3 R–Cl + H₃PO₃. PBr₃ and PI₃ are made in situ from red P + Br₂ / I₂.
  • PCl₅: R–OH + PCl₅ → R–Cl + POCl₃ + HCl.
  • SOCl₂: R–OH + SOCl₂ → R–Cl + SO₂↑ + HCl↑: both gases escape, so the product is pure. The best reagent.
  • Not for phenol: its C–O bond has partial double-bond character, so aryl halides cannot be made this way.

The one rule

R–OH → R–X

In every method the OH group of the alcohol is replaced by a halogen. What changes from reagent to reagent is the by-products, and that decides how pure the alkyl halide is.

1. Halogen acids, HX

R–OH + HCl —(anhydrous ZnCl₂)→ R–Cl + H₂O

  • 1° and 2° alcohols need anhydrous ZnCl₂; dry HCl gas is passed through the alcohol, or the alcohol is heated in conc. aqueous HCl.
  • 3° alcohols react on simply shaking with conc. HCl at room temperature.
  • Order of reactivity with a given HX: 3° > 2° > 1°.
  • Alkyl bromide: constant-boiling HBr (48%), or NaBr + H₂SO₄. Alkyl iodide: heat with NaI or KI in 95% H₃PO₄.

2. Phosphorus trihalides

3 R–OH + PCl₃ → 3 R–Cl + H₃PO₃

One PCl₃ reacts with three alcohol molecules. PBr₃ and PI₃ are generated in situ from red phosphorus and bromine or iodine.

3. Phosphorus pentachloride

R–OH + PCl₅ → R–Cl + POCl₃ + HCl

4. Thionyl chloride: the best

R–OH + SOCl₂ → R–Cl + SO₂↑ + HCl↑

Both by-products are gases that escape, so the alkyl chloride is left pure. That is why SOCl₂ is preferred.

Common mistake: phenol

Phenol does not give chlorobenzene with HCl, PCl₃, PCl₅ or SOCl₂. In phenol the C–O bond has partial double-bond character (resonance with the ring), so it is stronger than a single bond and is not broken. These methods do not make aryl halides.

More from Haloalkanes and Haloarenes

The Polar C–X Bond: Polarity, Bond Length and StrengthThe C–X bond is polar: the halogen pulls the shared pair, so carbon is δ+ and the halogen δ−, and nucleophiles attack that carbon. From F to I the halogen gets bigger, so the C–X bond gets longer (139 → 214 pm) and weaker (452 → 234 kJ mol⁻¹). That is why R–I reacts fastest and R–F slowest, even though fluorine is the most electronegative halogen; and why CH₃Cl, not CH₃F, has the largest dipole moment (μ = q × d).ChemistryOne Atom, Many Lives: Haloalkanes and HaloarenesIn 1847 James Simpson found that chloroform, CHCl₃, could put people to sleep for surgery. Replace a hydrogen of a hydrocarbon with a halogen and you get a haloalkane (halogen on an sp³ alkyl carbon) or a haloarene (halogen on an sp² ring carbon). They are everywhere: chloramphenicol for typhoid, thyroxine with four iodine atoms, chloroquine for malaria, halothane for anaesthesia. The polar C–X bond, with a δ+ carbon, is why they react, and it opens the whole chapter.Chemistry · 9:16 + 16:9Count the Halogens: Mono, Di, Poly, Gem and VicHalogen compounds are classified first by the number of halogen atoms: one is mono, two is di, three or more is poly (tri-, tetra-). Count the halogens, not the carbons: chloroform, CHCl₃, has one carbon but three chlorines, so it is a polyhalogen compound. Dihalides split by position: both halogens on the same carbon is a gem-dihalide (alkylidene halide, e.g. ethylidene chloride, 1,1-dichloroethane); on adjacent carbons a vic-dihalide (alkylene dihalide, e.g. ethylene dichloride, 1,2-dichloroethane).ChemistryAlkyl, Vinyl, Allyl and Aryl Halides: Which Carbon Holds X?Alkyl, vinyl (vinylic), allyl (allylic) and aryl halides differ only in the carbon that holds the halogen. Alkyl: a saturated sp³ carbon (1°, 2°, 3° by the alkyl groups on it). Vinyl: X directly on a carbon of a C=C (sp²). Allyl: X on the sp³ carbon next to a C=C. Aryl: X directly on a carbon of an aromatic ring (sp²), e.g. chlorobenzene. Bonus: X on the sp³ CH₂ next to a benzene ring is a benzyl (benzylic) halide. Identify the carbon attached to X: that's the key.ChemistryNaming Haloalkanes and Haloarenes: Common and IUPAC NamesEvery halide has two correct names. The common name is the alkyl group + halide (isopropyl chloride, ethyl chloride). The IUPAC name writes the halogen as a prefix (fluoro, chloro, bromo, iodo) on the longest chain, numbered from the end that gives the lowest locants; when both ends tie, alphabetical order decides (2-bromo-3-methylbutane). On benzene one halogen keeps the same name (chlorobenzene); two use o-, m-, p- in common names and 1,2 / 1,3 / 1,4 in IUPAC.Chemistry
