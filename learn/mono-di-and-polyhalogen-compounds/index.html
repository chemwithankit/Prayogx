Count the Halogens: Mono, Di, Poly, Gem and Vic

Video not playing? YouTube

Halogen compounds are classified first by the number of halogen atoms: one is mono, two is di, three or more is poly (tri-, tetra-). Count the halogens, not the carbons: chloroform, CHCl₃, has one carbon but three chlorines, so it is a polyhalogen compound. Dihalides split by position: both halogens on the same carbon is a gem-dihalide (alkylidene halide, e.g. ethylidene chloride, 1,1-dichloroethane); on adjacent carbons a vic-dihalide (alkylene dihalide, e.g. ethylene dichloride, 1,2-dichloroethane).

Key points

  • Count the halogens, not the carbons: one X = mono, two = di, three or more = poly (tri-, tetra-).
  • Same rule for haloarenes: C₆H₅Cl is a monohaloarene, C₆H₄Cl₂ a dihaloarene.
  • The CHCl₃ trap: one carbon but three Cl, so chloroform is trihalo, a polyhalogen compound.
  • Gem-dihalide: both X on the same carbon (gemini = twins), e.g. CH₃CHCl₂; common name alkylidene halide (ethylidene chloride).
  • Vic-dihalide: X on adjacent carbons (vicinal = neighbour), e.g. ClCH₂CH₂Cl; common name alkylene dihalide (ethylene dichloride).
  • IUPAC: both are dihaloalkanes; locant 1,1 = gem, 1,2 = vic.

More from Haloalkanes and Haloarenes

One Atom, Many Lives: Haloalkanes and HaloarenesIn 1847 James Simpson found that chloroform, CHCl₃, could put people to sleep for surgery. Replace a hydrogen of a hydrocarbon with a halogen and you get a haloalkane (halogen on an sp³ alkyl carbon) or a haloarene (halogen on an sp² ring carbon). They are everywhere: chloramphenicol for typhoid, thyroxine with four iodine atoms, chloroquine for malaria, halothane for anaesthesia. The polar C–X bond, with a δ+ carbon, is why they react, and it opens the whole chapter.Chemistry · 9:16 + 16:9Naming Haloalkanes and Haloarenes: Common and IUPAC NamesEvery halide has two correct names. The common name is the alkyl group + halide (isopropyl chloride, ethyl chloride). The IUPAC name writes the halogen as a prefix (fluoro, chloro, bromo, iodo) on the longest chain, numbered from the end that gives the lowest locants; when both ends tie, alphabetical order decides (2-bromo-3-methylbutane). On benzene one halogen keeps the same name (chlorobenzene); two use o-, m-, p- in common names and 1,2 / 1,3 / 1,4 in IUPAC.ChemistryThe Polar C–X Bond: Polarity, Bond Length and StrengthThe C–X bond is polar: the halogen pulls the shared pair, so carbon is δ+ and the halogen δ−, and nucleophiles attack that carbon. From F to I the halogen gets bigger, so the C–X bond gets longer (139 → 214 pm) and weaker (452 → 234 kJ mol⁻¹). That is why R–I reacts fastest and R–F slowest, even though fluorine is the most electronegative halogen; and why CH₃Cl, not CH₃F, has the largest dipole moment (μ = q × d).ChemistryHaloalkanes from Alcohols: HX, PCl₃, PCl₅ and SOCl₂One rule: R–OH → R–X, the OH group is replaced by a halogen; only the reagent and its by-products change. HX gives water (anhydrous ZnCl₂ for 1° and 2° alcohols; reactivity 3° > 2° > 1°), PCl₃ gives H₃PO₃ (1 PCl₃ : 3 R–OH), PCl₅ gives POCl₃ + HCl, and SOCl₂ gives SO₂ + HCl, two gases that escape, so the alkyl chloride is pure. Phenol does not react this way: its C–O bond has partial double-bond character.ChemistryFree Radical Mechanism: Halogenation of AlkanesButane + Cl₂ in UV light gives 1-chlorobutane and 2-chlorobutane, because butane has primary and secondary hydrogens. The free-radical chain has three stages: initiation (Cl₂ splits homolytically into 2 Cl•), propagation (Cl• abstracts an H to give HCl and a butyl radical, which takes Cl from Cl₂ and releases a new Cl•) and termination (two radicals combine). The yield of any one product is low: isomers and polychloro products form a mixture that is hard to separate.ChemistryMarkovnikov's Rule: Addition of HX to AlkenesPropene + HI gives 2-iodopropane (major) and 1-iodopropane (minor). In step 1 the π electrons of C=C move to the H of HI, forming a C–H bond on the CH₂ carbon, while the H–I pair goes to iodine (I⁻); the + is left on the middle carbon, a secondary carbocation. A 2° carbocation is more stable than a 1° one, so that pathway is favoured, and I⁻ then bonds to it. Markovnikov's rule: H goes to the double-bond carbon with more H atoms, the halogen to the one with fewer.ChemistryAddition of Halogens to Alkenes: the Bromine TestEthene + Br₂ in CCl₄ gives BrCH₂–CH₂Br: the π bond breaks and one Br adds to each double-bond carbon, giving a vic-dibromide. The reddish-brown colour of bromine disappears, so the reaction is the lab test for unsaturation. Propene gives 1,2-dibromopropane and cyclohexene gives 1,2-dibromocyclohexane. Benzene fails the test: adding Br₂ would destroy its aromatic stability, so the colour stays.ChemistryNCERT Example 6.3: Monochloro Structural Isomers of 2-MethylbutaneFree radical monochlorination of (CH₃)₂CHCH₂CH₃ (2-methylbutane) gives 4 monochloro structural isomers, not 5: the two CH₃ groups on the CH carbon are equivalent, so the molecule has only 4 kinds of hydrogen. Replacing one H of each kind gives 1-chloro-3-methylbutane, 2-chloro-3-methylbutane, 2-chloro-2-methylbutane and 1-chloro-2-methylbutane. Counting stereoisomers (JEE), the total is 6.ChemistryHalogen Exchange: Finkelstein and Swarts ReactionsHalogen exchange (NCERT 6.4.3) swaps the Cl or Br of an alkyl halide for another halogen. Finkelstein: R–X + NaI in dry acetone gives R–I; NaCl / NaBr is insoluble in dry acetone and precipitates, so by Le Chatelier's principle the reaction goes forward. Swarts: heating R–Cl / R–Br with a heavy metallic fluoride (AgF, Hg₂F₂, CoF₂ or SbF₃) gives the alkyl fluoride. Trick: Finkelstein → iodide, Swarts → fluoride.ChemistryHaloarenes by Electrophilic Substitution: Reaction and MechanismAryl chlorides and bromides are made by electrophilic substitution: C₆H₆ + Br₂ → C₆H₅Br + HBr with FeBr₃ (or Fe) in the dark, and Cl₂ / FeCl₃ gives chlorobenzene. FeBr₃ (a Lewis acid) turns Br₂ into the electrophile Br⁺; Br⁺ attacks the ring to form the arenium ion (σ-complex), whose + charge is spread over the ortho and para carbons; loss of H⁺ restores aromaticity and regenerates the catalyst. Iodination needs an oxidising agent (HNO₃ / HIO₄); fluorine is too reactive.ChemistryName and Classify Haloalkanes: NCERT Questions SolvedThree NCERT exercise questions solved with one checklist: longest chain (look inside the branches), lowest locants, alphabetical order on a tie; and the class (1°, 2°, 3°) is decided only by the carbon that carries the halogen.
