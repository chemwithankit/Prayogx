Why Is Ice Slippery?

Video not playing? YouTube

Not because your weight melts it: that lowers the melting point by only a fraction of a degree. A thin liquid-like surface layer (premelting) and frictional melting do the work, and scientists still debate the details.

Key points

  • Pressure melting is the old idea, but a person's weight lowers ice's melting point by only a fraction of a degree, and ice is slippery even near −30 °C.
  • Friction: a sliding skate or shoe heats the surface and melts a thin water film.
  • Premelting (Faraday): a very thin liquid-like layer covers ice even below 0 °C.
  • Newer research suggests loosely bound surface molecules that roll like ball bearings; the full answer is still debated.

Why pressure is not enough

Pressure lowers ice's melting point by only about 0.0074 °C per atmosphere. Even on a thin skate blade, a person's weight shifts it by a fraction of a degree, yet ice stays slippery at temperatures far below that, around −30 °C.

Still an open question

Experiments show water molecules at the ice surface are loosely bound and mobile. Some researchers describe them as tiny ball bearings; how much each mechanism contributes is still being studied. Science keeps some questions open, and that is part of the fun.
