Additivity and Conservation of ChargeRub a glass rod with silk and the two get charges that are always exactly equal and opposite. Rubbing moves electrons from the glass to the silk; it never makes charge. Charge is additive (the total is the algebraic sum, signs included) and conserved (the total charge of an isolated system never changes). The rule holds even when particles transform: a free neutron becomes a proton, an electron and an antineutrino, and a gamma photon near a nucleus becomes an electron and a positron, with the total charge unchanged every time.Electric Charges and Fields · PhysicsWhy a Comb Stays Charged but a Spoon Doesn't: Conductors, Insulators and EarthingRub a plastic comb on dry hair and it lifts paper bits; rub a steel spoon and nothing happens. Rubbing moves electrons, and what happens next depends on the material. Conductors (metals, the human body, the earth) have free electrons, so charge spreads over them and leaks away through the hand and body into the earth. Insulators (plastic, glass, nylon, wood) hold their electrons bound, so charge stays where it was put. Earthing connects a body to the earth, a giant charge reservoir; at home the earth wire and fuse use it for safety, and fuel tankers are earthed to prevent static sparks.Electric Charges and Fields · Physics
