Key points

  • Rubbing transfers electrons: a plastic comb run through dry hair gains electrons and becomes negative.
  • Two kinds of charge: like charges repel, unlike charges attract.
  • A charged body attracts a neutral one by induction: the nearer opposite charge wins.
  • Coulomb's law F = kq₁q₂/r²: double the distance and the force becomes one-fourth. Each charge sets up an electric field; field lines start on + and end on −.

Why the paper jumps

  • Rubbing moves electrons from the hair to the comb: the comb becomes negatively charged, the hair positively.
  • The paper is neutral, but near the comb its charges shift slightly: positive charge nearer the comb, negative charge farther away.
  • The nearer (attractive) charge feels a stronger force than the farther (repulsive) one, so the net force pulls the paper to the comb. This is induction.

Coulomb's law

F = k q₁q₂ / r², k = 1/(4πε₀) ≈ 9 × 10⁹ N m² C⁻²

Electric field and field lines

A charge q sets up an electric field E around it; another charge q₀ placed there feels F = q₀E. The field is how a force acts without contact.

  • Field lines start on positive charges and end on negative charges.
  • The tangent to a field line gives the direction of E; closer lines mean a stronger field.
  • Two field lines never cross.
